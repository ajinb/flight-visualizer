Why flights aren't straight lines

Pick two US airports. The map draws the shortest possible path (the great circle) next to the route air traffic control actually prefers, which follows airways between navigation fixes. The gap between them is the cost of a shared, structured sky.

Educational only. Not for flight planning or navigation. Data comes from public FAA sources and may be out of date or incomplete; always use current official charts and briefings for real flights.

    How it works

    1

    Build the data

    A script turns the FAA's 28-day navigation data (fixes, navaids, airways, preferred routes) into small JSON files. No server runs when you visit this page.

    2

    Expand the route

    A preferred route like GLL J60 BOS is a list of tokens. Fixes become points; an airway token becomes every point between its entry and exit; a SID or STAR becomes its published points.

    3

    Or search for one

    Where no preferred route exists, A* search over the airway network finds a short path. It is a teaching model: it ignores one-way airways and altitude limits.

    Data and limits

    Loading data details…

    • FAA NASR 28-Day Subscription: airports, fixes, navaids, airways, SIDs and STARs.
    • FAA Preferred Routes database (published through NASR): the routes shown as "preferred".
    • Airport seed data: OurAirports (public domain), used until the FAA data has been built.
    • Map tiles © OpenStreetMap contributors. Mapping library: Leaflet (BSD-2).

    Departure and arrival procedures (SIDs and STARs) are drawn along the transition the route names, using the runway branch nearest the airport. Their altitude and speed restrictions are not modelled.
